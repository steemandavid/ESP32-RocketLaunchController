Operating procedures, safe-ignition method and complete safety-feature reference for the two-unit wireless launch controller.

Read before use

This system fires pyrotechnic igniters. It is a personally built controller that has not completed environmental and field hardening, and it is supplied without an enclosure-level certification of any kind. Operate it only under your club’s and your national safety code, under the authority of a Range Safety Officer, and only after you have verified your own build against the functional specification.

1About this manual

This manual describes how to operate the ESP32 Wireless Rocket Launch Controller safely: how to set it up, how to fire an igniter for a launch or a static motor test, what every indicator means, and what the system does when something goes wrong.

1.1 Who this manual is for

Two roles are referenced throughout, and they may or may not be the same person depending on your club’s practice:

RoleResponsibility
LCO
Launch Control Officer		 Runs the whole operation, single-handed. Checks the rockets, motors and igniters at the pad; checks and arms the base unit; arms the remote; fires every shot from the remote; and afterwards returns both keys to SAFE and releases the range to the participants. Both keys live on the LCO’s lanyard and nobody else ever touches them. The LCO owns every abort decision from the moment the pad is armed.
Range Safety OfficerAuthorises the launch, sets the safe distances, and settles any question this manual does not. The RSO’s instruction always overrides this document.

1.2 How instructions are marked

Danger

An action or condition that can cause an unintended ignition, injury or death. Never work around one of these.

Caution

An action or condition that can damage equipment, corrupt a test, or put the system into a state that needs a walk to the pad to clear.

Note

Background on why the system behaves the way it does — useful for interpreting an indication in the field.

1.3 Applicability and version matching

This manual documents firmware 1.2.1. Both units run one codebase and check each other’s version at link-up; a mismatch in any digit of MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH refuses the link outright and the remote shows a FIRMWARE MISMATCH screen. There is no partial-compatibility mode by design — the two units share timing constants such as the pre-fire delay, and a remote counting down from a different number than the base is a launch hazard, not an inconvenience.

Caution

Never take a unit to the field that was flashed alone. After flashing, reset the unit that was flashed first — the version check is latched, so it will otherwise sit refusing to link even once its peer is up to date.

Danger

A unit flashed with the --inject fault-injection build deliberately reports false status and must never be used with a live igniter.

A fault-injection remote is unmistakable: its boot splash carries a red frame around the whole screen and a red !! FAULT INJECTION BUILD — NOT SAFE FOR LIVE USE !! block where the club credit normally sits. If you see it, stop and reflash with a stock build.

A fault-injection base has no such tell on the remote. The base has no display, and it does not advertise its build over the link, so the remote cannot warn you about it — it announces itself only on a developer’s terminal. Treat the provenance of the base firmware as something you verify at the bench, not something the remote will tell you at the pad.

2System overview

2.1 The two units

The controller is two independent battery-powered units joined only by a radio link. There is no wire between the firing point and the pad.

UnitWhere it livesWhat it does
Base unit At the launch pad or test stand, wired directly to up to eight igniter circuits. Owns the fire path. Measures igniter continuity, holds the physical arm key switch, energises the arm and channel relays, sounds the pad siren. The base is authoritative — it decides whether ignition happens.
Remote unit Carried by the launch operator at the firing point, roughly 200 m away. Selects the channel, requests arming, and holds the dead-man fire button. Displays pad status on a 480×320 colour screen. The remote can only ask; it cannot force.

3.1 Remote unit

3.2 Base unit

Control / indicatorFunction
Base arm key switch The brass-barrel key switch on the red plate at the right-hand end of the channel row — the on/off toggle plate sits directly above it. An SPDT key switch wired in series with the arm relay coil. In SAFE it physically breaks the coil circuit, so the arm relay cannot energise no matter what the firmware does. This is the LCO’s control, and the key lives on the lanyard, not in the switch.
Green lamp “SAFE” On the key plate, engraved SAFE at the upper left of the barrel. Lit when the base arm key is in SAFE. Powered directly from the battery through the switch contacts — it works with the ESP32 crashed, hung or unpowered.
Red lamp “ARM” On the key plate, engraved ARM at the upper right of the barrel. Lit when the base arm key is in ARM. Also passive.
Red lamp “HOT” (arm relay live) On the key plate, engraved HOT at the lower left of the barrel. Wired across the arm relay coil. Lit only when the base arm key and the firmware are both driving the relay — i.e. when battery voltage is on the fire bus. Also passive. Treat this lamp as the definitive statement that the pad is live.
Siren Mounted inside the case, behind the plate — you hear it, you don’t see it. Sounds continuously from arming through the end of the fire pulse. Short patterns signal link loss (4 × 500 ms), an error state (3 × 200 ms) and a continuity-loss disarm (3 × 200 ms). A single 200 ms chirp at the end of boot says the unit came up healthy and the siren itself has just been proven.
LED strip Eight pixels, one per channel, showing live locally-measured continuity. Mounted on the plate: each channel's IGN lens is its pixel (the FIRE lens beside it is separate). Channel 1 is at the data-in end on the base.
Channel modules CH1–CH8 Eight identical red modules along the bottom of the plate, numbered CH1–CH8 from the left. Each carries indicator lenses at the IGN and FIRE markings and a yellow XT60 socket for the igniter clips — the IGN lens is that channel's NeoPixel pixel and shows the continuity colours of §3.5, the FIRE lens is the relay indicator — and CH1 is the leftmost module, not the rightmost. When idle, each channel’s relay rests on its NC contact, which routes the igniter to the continuity sense circuit rather than the fire bus.
On/off switch The silver toggle on the small red plate directly above the key plate, engraved ON above and OFF below. Wired inline with the battery. It physically cuts pack power when off. This is the switch to reach for when a base unit must be killed — see the danger note in §5.11.
SMA antenna connector External antenna for the 2.4 GHz link, on a bulkhead at the far left of the plate at mid-height. Never power the unit up without the antenna fitted. Antenna height and clear line of sight do far more for range than anything in the firmware.
USB port The ESP32-S3’s native USB, on the JTAG socket at the top left of the plate; the service cables pass through a grommet in the case lid. Firmware flashing and the serial console. Not used in normal operation.
COM port The ESP32-S3’s UART bridge, on the COM socket at the top left of the plate, beside the JTAG socket. Either socket serves the serial console and flashing; not used in normal operation.
Charger connector Balance connector for the 3S pack. Not brought out on the as-built front plate — charge the pack by opening the case, and switch the unit off before charging.
Why the passive LEDs matter

Every other indicator in the system is a claim made by software. The three LEDs on the base are the only ones made by copper. When you walk to the pad, they are what you believe.

3.4 The status band

A coloured band runs along the bottom of every screen. It reports the state of the fire path and nothing else, so it can be read across a launch site without reading any text. Learn this table before you learn anything else in this manual.

3.5 Continuity indication

The base measures each igniter circuit with a test current of no more than 1 mA and sorts the result into three bands. Shape carries the meaning as well as colour, so the grid stays readable regardless of colour vision.

GlyphBandApproximate resistanceWhat it means
●CONNECTEDbelow ~67 ΩCurrent can flow. It does not mean the igniter is sound — a dead short reads the same. Arming is permitted.
▲MARGINAL~67–500 ΩA high-resistance connection. Arming is permitted but you should re-seat the clips before firing.
○OPENabove ~500 ΩNo usable circuit. Arming is refused, and an armed channel going OPEN disarms the pad automatically.
Note

A full sweep of all eight channels takes about 800 ms, so a change at the pad takes up to a second to appear on the remote. After a fire pulse, OPEN on the fired channel is the expected and desired reading.

4Setting up

4.1 Site layout and distances

The controller does not set your safe distance — your national code and your RSO do, from the total installed impulse. The controller’s job is to let you keep that distance. Lay the site out so that:

  • the firing point is at or beyond the code distance for the largest motor on the pad, and within the link’s working range (design target ~200 m, measured 200 m line of sight);
  • the operator has an unobstructed view of the pad — near the ground, radio range is limited far more by terrain than by transmit power, so height and clear line of sight matter more than anything you can change in software;
  • the base unit sits where its siren can be heard across the whole pad area, and where its three status LEDs are visible on approach;
  • igniter leads run away from the base, not under it, and never alongside mains or generator cabling.

4.2 Powering up the base

  1. Confirm the base arm key switch is in SAFE and remove the key. Do this before connecting the battery, every time. Expect Green SAFE LED off (no power yet).
  2. Confirm no igniters are connected. Igniters are the last thing connected, not the first — see §4.5.
  3. Connect the 3S pack. Nothing should come alive yet — the on/off switch is still off.
  4. Flip the on/off switch to ON. Expect Green SAFE LED lights immediately and stays lit. The strip runs a cyan chase, then settles into the channel map once the first continuity sweep completes. Stop if the ARM RELAY LIVE LED lights at any point with the base arm key in SAFE. That is a wiring fault in the fire path. Disconnect the battery and do not use the unit.
  5. Let the boot self-tests complete. The base runs twelve self-test suites at boot — struct packing, CRC vectors, ADC calibration, radio setup. A failure puts it straight into ERROR with three siren blasts. Expect A single short chirp once boot completes — the “I’m alive and the siren works” pulse — and no red triple-flash on the strip. Three blasts instead of the chirp means boot failed.

4.3 Powering up the remote and linking

  1. Confirm the remote arm key switch is in SAFE.
  2. Connect the 2S pack. Nothing should come alive yet — the on/off switch is still off.
  3. Flip the on/off switch to ON. Expect Splash screen, then “Connecting to base…” with an attempt counter.
  4. Wait for the link. Linking normally completes in well under a second; the splash is held for 10 s so it stays readable. Expect Main status screen, LINK OK in the top bar, a green SAFE status band, and both battery voltages shown. Stop if you see FIRMWARE MISMATCH — the units are not on the same build (§1.3), and nothing will operate until they are.

4.4 Pre-operation acceptance checks

Run these once per session, before any igniter is connected. They take about a minute and they are the difference between trusting the system and hoping. Checks 1–6 are run with the remote in hand at the base; 7 and 8 are run after stepping back to the chosen remote firing point, because both are tests of distance.

#CheckPass criterion
1Firmware versions matchNo mismatch screen; both units link.
2Battery statesBase comfortably above 10.5 V, remote above 7.0 V. Below those thresholds the system refuses to arm — find that out now, not at the pad.
3Status band tracks both arm keysTurn the base arm key to ARM: band goes yellow. Turn the remote arm key to ARMED: band goes orange READY TO ARM. Return both to safe: band returns green. Never grey while linked.
4Passive LEDs are honestBase arm key SAFE → green only. Base arm key ARM → red KEY LED only. ARM RELAY LIVE must stay dark in both positions while nothing is armed.
5Fire button ringGreen in idle. It must not go red for a press in IDLE.
6Continuity reads OPENAll eight channels ○ OPEN with nothing connected. A channel reading CONNECTED with no igniter on it is a short in the harness.
7Siren audibilityConfirm the siren is heard clearly across the whole pad area, and again from the remote firing point.
8Link quality at the firing pointWalk to the remote firing point and check LINK OK, not LINK WEAK. A degraded link refuses arming by design.

4.5 Connecting igniters

Danger

The base arm key switch is in SAFE, and the key is out of the switch and around the LCO’s neck, before anyone touches an igniter lead. Both keys are carried on a lanyard by the LCO at all times; nobody else ever holds them. There is no step in this manual where an igniter is connected while the base arm key is in ARM.

  1. Confirm SAFE at the base. Green LED lit, ARM RELAY LIVE dark, key removed.
  2. Confirm SAFE on the remote. Status band green, SAFE. If it reads grey, the remote does not know the pad state — resolve that before approaching.
  3. Short the igniter leads together until connected. Standard practice, and unaffected by anything the controller does.
  4. Connect one channel at a time and watch the strip. The channel’s pixel and the remote’s grid should step to ● CONNECTED within about a second. Expect ● CONNECTED on the connected channel, ○ OPEN on all others. Investigate if the wrong channel changes — that is a harness mis-mapping, and it means you would fire a motor you did not select.
  5. Note the channel numbers. Write down which channel is which rocket or motor. The controller shows you a number; only you know what it is wired to.
  6. Leave the pad, keys on the lanyard. The keys stay with the LCO. They are never handed over, never left in a switch, and never put down.

5Safety

This section is the core of the manual. It sets out the principle the system is built on, catalogues every safety feature in the hardware, the firmware and the radio link, and then gives the procedures for performing an ignition safely — for a rocket launch and for a static motor test — together with the abort and misfire drills and an honest account of what the system does not protect against.

5.1 Safety principles

Four rules shape every design decision in the controller. Knowing them makes the system’s behaviour predictable in the field, including its refusals.

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
No single fault fires an igniter Defence in depth. The fire path crosses two independent relay breaks, and the first of those cannot even be commanded unless a physical key is turned. No software bug, and no single failed component, is sufficient on its own.
Deliberate, multi-step human action Ignition requires the base arm key turned at the pad, the remote arm key turned at the firing point, a deliberate half-second button hold, and then a fire button held continuously — four separate acts, and the first is performed at the pad while the last is performed at the firing point. There is no single control that fires anything.
Fail safe, fail loud Every anomaly — link loss, low battery, a lead falling off, an invalid message — results in disarming, not in continuing. Inputs are wired active-low, so a broken wire reads as “safe”, “disarmed”, “button released”. And every refusal makes a noise and puts a message on screen; a refusal you cannot perceive is indistinguishable from an input that never registered, and the natural response to that is to try again.
Do not self-heal A genuinely unexpected fault — a welded relay, two channels armed at once — latches the unit into ERROR, which is only cleared by walking to it and cycling power. A controller that quietly recovers from a fault it cannot explain is a controller you cannot reason about.

5.2 The fire path: two independent breaks

Current can only reach an igniter if two relays in series are closed at the same time: the arm relay and the selected channel’s relay. Neither one alone can complete the circuit.

The base arm key switch is not in the fire path itself. It sits in the arm relay’s coil drive path, in series with a firmware-driven MOSFET, so it governs whether the arm relay may close at all. That is a different kind of protection from a contact in the fire path, and a stronger one: with the key in SAFE the arm relay cannot be energised by any software fault whatsoever, because the coil has no current available to it.

The hardware AND gate

The arm relay’s coil is fed through the base arm key switch and returned through a firmware-driven MOSFET. Both must be active for the relay to pull in. A software fault that drives the MOSFET continuously achieves nothing with the base arm key in SAFE; the key left in ARM achieves nothing without the firmware. This has been verified electrically at the arm-sense node, not merely inferred from the indicator LEDs.

This is why the fire path counts two breaks rather than three, and why that is not a weakening. The base arm key does not merely add another contact in series — it removes the possibility of the first break moving at all.

5.3 Hardware safety features

FeatureWhat it protects against
Two independent breaks in the fire pathAny single closed contact, stuck output or software fault. Both are relays, and they are commanded by different parts of the firmware at different times — the arm relay at arming, the channel relay only during the pulse itself.
Hardware AND gate on the arm relay coilFirmware energising the fire bus on its own, or the base arm key left in ARM doing so on its own. The base arm key switch and the MOSFET are in series in the coil path, so both are required before the first break in the fire path can even move.
Fail-safe relay defaultsBoot, brown-out and crash. All relays rest de-energised; 10 kΩ gate pull-downs hold every MOSFET off while the ESP32’s pins are still high-impedance, before a single instruction of firmware has run.
Relay outputs configured first at bootA crash during later initialisation. Relay GPIOs are driven safe before any other peripheral is touched.
Passive, battery-powered status LEDsA dead, crashed or unpowered microcontroller. The SAFE / KEY ARM / ARM RELAY LIVE lamps run from the battery through the switch and relay contacts and require no GPIO at all.
Current-limited continuity sensingThe measurement itself igniting the device. Test current is capped at ≈1 mA by two fusible resistors in series — 1.5 kΩ and 1.3 kΩ — so a single shorted resistor does not remove the limit.
Igniter routed to sense, not to power, at restAn energised bus reaching a resting channel. The channel relay’s NC contact physically disconnects the igniter from the fire bus whenever the relay is not driven.
Arm-sense feedback on the fire busA relay that did not close, and a relay that will not open. The same input verifies arming and detects welded contacts.
Active-low inputs throughoutBroken wires and unplugged connectors. A disconnected fire button reads “released”; a disconnected arm switch reads “disarmed”.
Snubbers and clamps on every channelRelay-arc energy coupling back into the microcontroller’s sense inputs. All eight channels carry RC snubbers, dual Schottky clamps and a sense-branch series resistor.
Hardware brown-out detectorUnder-voltage operation with unpredictable GPIO states. A brown-out resets to the safe state of the row above.

5.4 Firmware safety features

The arming guards

Every one of these nine must pass before the base will energise the arm relay. Any failure produces a NACK, a message on the remote and a beep — never a silent refusal.

#GuardRefused because
1Base arm key switch physically in ARMRead from a dedicated sense line, not assumed. The key’s position is verified independently of the relay it controls.
2Selected channel has continuityAn OPEN circuit will not fire; arming it only creates a live bus with nothing to consume it.
3Channel number in range 1–8A corrupt or malicious channel field cannot address anything.
4No other channel already armedSingle-channel arming. Two live channels is a fault, not a feature.
5Message integrity CRC validA corrupted or forged frame is discarded rather than acted on.
6Session token matchesA command from a previous session cannot be honoured in this one.
7Sequence number is newerA previously captured ARM or FIRE cannot be replayed.
8Base battery above 10.5 VA pack that sags under relay and igniter load is an unreliable fire path.
9Link quality acceptable (≤30 % ping loss over 10)Arming on a degraded link invites a false dead-man abort mid-countdown.

The remote applies its own guards before it even transmits: its arm switch must be on, its own battery above 7.0 V, its cached pad status no more than 4 s old, and the link not degraded.

After the guards pass, one confirmation follows: the base energises the arm relay and requires the arm-sense line to confirm the contacts actually closed within 200 ms. If it does not, the relay is de-energised again and the arm is refused. A single miss is a retryable refusal — the window is genuinely tight for an ageing relay. Two consecutive misses are treated as a wiring or relay failure and latch the base into ERROR, because at that point the fire path can no longer be confirmed.

Continuous monitoring while armed

FeatureBehaviour
Continuity watched throughout Continuity is not merely checked at the instant of arming. If the armed channel goes OPEN — a clip falls off, someone pulls a lead — the base disarms itself and silences the siren within about a second, and the remote sounds a distinctive three-beep pattern.
Dead-man switch While firing, the remote repeats a FIRE message every 200 ms. If the base does not receive one within 500 ms — button released, operator down, link gone — it cuts power. The button’s release is debounced faster than its press (20 ms against 80 ms) because a missed release fires an igniter the operator has let go of, while a spurious release merely aborts.
Auto-disarm after 10 s An armed pad with no fire command times out and disarms itself. You cannot arm and walk away.
Auto-disarm after firing The pulse ends, all relays de-energise, the channel is cleared. Firing again requires the full arming sequence from the beginning.
Fire pulse backstop An independent maximum-duration check synthesises pulse completion 250 ms past the nominal 1 s, so a lost timer notification cannot leave a channel energised.
Welded-contact detection If the arm sense reads live while the relay is commanded off, the base latches ERROR immediately — current can reach the fire bus with nothing commanding it.
Battery guards, biased safe Each reading is the median of a 33-sample burst rather than a mean. A sample clipped at the ADC ceiling can only bias a mean upward, i.e. make a flat pack read healthy — the one direction a battery guard must never fail in.
Watchdogs A 5 s task watchdog covers every critical task individually, not just total system lockup. A watchdog reset boots to the safe state and reports itself to the remote.
Priority-ordered tasks The arm-switch monitor and the fire button run above the state machines, which run above the display, buzzer and LEDs. A busy screen can never delay a release being noticed.
Boot self-tests Twelve suites at power-up, including byte-offset verification of every wire message and a CRC test vector. A toolchain that would silently mis-parse a fire command is caught before the radio is started.
Terminal ERROR state Welded relay, multiple channels armed, internal assertion, critical battery: the unit halts, relays safe, siren blasts, and stays halted until someone walks to it and cycles power.

5.5 Communication security

The radio link carries commands that fire pyrotechnics, so it is protected in three independent layers:

#LayerProtects against
1AES-128-CCM encryption (ESP-NOW)External devices injecting or reading commands.
2Application CRC32-C integrity check over the header and payload with a pre-shared keyCorrupted or forged frames being acted on.
3Replay protection — monotonic sequence numbers plus a random session token issued at each link-upA previously captured FIRE command being replayed later.

Both units must also be running byte-identical firmware, or the link is refused outright.

5.6 Fail-safe behaviour matrix

What the system does when something goes wrong. In every row the answer is a variation on “de-energise everything and say so”.

ConditionSystem response

5.7 Rules for the pad

The controller enforces what it can. These are the rules it cannot enforce, and they matter more than any of its interlocks.

Danger — the four absolutes

1. Nobody approaches the pad while the status band reads ARM RELAY LIVE, while the siren is sounding, or while the base’s ARM RELAY LIVE lamp is lit.
2. Both keys live on the LCO’s lanyard, and the base key is removed from the switch whenever anyone is at the pad.
3. The LCO does not arm until the pad is confirmed clear by voice, and does not fire until the range is confirmed clear.
4. A grey STATUS UNKNOWN band is not permission to approach. It means the system cannot tell you anything.

  • Safe distances come from your code, not from this manual. Set them from the total installed impulse, per NAR, Tripoli or your national body, and let the RSO have the final word.
  • The pre-fire delay is agreed in advance. The default is 5 s. If you change it, both units must be reflashed together, since the remote runs its own countdown against the same constant.
  • Announce the count. The siren tells the pad; your voice tells the range.
  • One armed channel, one motor, one operator. The system enforces single-channel arming; do not defeat it with parallel wiring on a single channel unless the RSO has explicitly approved the cluster and the current budget.
  • Fire extinguishing equipment appropriate to the propellant is at hand, and someone other than the operator is watching for grass fires.

5.8 Safe ignition — rocket launch

This is the full procedure, from a set-up-and-checked system to a fired motor. Steps in red are the irreversible ones.

One person, in sequence

The LCO performs every step below alone, in order: the pad work first, then the walk back to the firing point, then the shot. The sequence is written so that no step requires a second pair of hands and no step is safe to perform out of order. Both keys stay on the LCO’s lanyard throughout.

Before you start

  • §4 setup and acceptance checks complete; igniters connected and the channel numbers written down.
  • Everyone clear of the pad; the LCO at the firing point with the remote and both keys.
  • RSO authorisation given.
  1. Confirm the system state before touching anything. Look at the remote: LINK OK, band green SAFE, both batteries healthy, fire ring green. Expect A green band and a link that is OK, not WEAK. Do not proceed if the band is grey. You have no reliable knowledge of the pad.
  2. Select the channel with the encoder. Turn until the cursor sits on the channel you wrote down for this rocket. Expect The cursor moves; the channel shows ● CONNECTED or ▲ MARGINAL. Do not proceed if it reads ○ OPEN — arming will be refused, and the igniter or the lead is the problem, not the controller.
  3. Call the pad clear and get an acknowledgement. Verbal, both ways. This is the last point at which a person can still be at the pad.
  4. Walk to the base and turn the arm key to ARM. Then step back from the base, take the key out if your switch allows it, and return to the firing point before doing anything else. Expect Base red KEY ARM lamp lit, green SAFE lamp out, ARM RELAY LIVE still dark. Remote band turns yellow. Stop if ARM RELAY LIVE lights now. Nothing has been armed yet, so the fire bus must not be live. Turn the key back to SAFE and take the unit out of service.
  5. At the firing point, turn the remote arm key to ARMED. Expect Band turns orange READY TO ARM, arm switch LED lit. Nothing at the pad has changed yet.
  6. Long-press the encoder button for 500 ms to arm. Hold it deliberately — about ⅔ of a second in total. A short press only shows a prompt. Expect Two short beeps, the ARMED screen with a large channel number, the band turns red ARM RELAY LIVE, the slow ~0.8 Hz armed tone begins, and the pad siren starts and stays on. At the base, ARM RELAY LIVE lights. If refused Three rapid beeps and a red toast naming the reason. Read it, fix it, and start again from step 1 — see §6.1.
  7. You now have 10 seconds. The arm times out and disarms itself if no fire command follows. If you need longer, disarm deliberately and re-arm when ready rather than racing the timer.
  8. Confirm the range is clear, then press and HOLD the fire button. Do not tap it. Do not rest your hand on it. Hold it as a decision you are continuously making. Expect Fire ring turns red, the buzzer steps up to the insistent ~4 Hz tone, and the 5 s countdown appears and decrements.
  9. Keep holding through the countdown and the pulse. At zero, the channel relay closes for a 1 s pulse. Releasing at any moment from step 8 onwards cuts current within a fraction of a second. Expect IGNITION ACTIVE, then the base disarms itself automatically.
  10. Release the button. Expect FIRE COMPLETE screen for 10 s with the igniter reading: ○ OPEN – LIKELY FIRED is the expected outcome. Siren stops, band returns to yellow or green, LED strip shows the fired channel flipping to OPEN.
  11. Fire the remaining pads, if any. Return to step 2 and repeat for the next channel. The remote stays armed and the base key stays in ARM between shots — but only while you remain at the firing point and nobody goes forward. If anyone needs to approach the pad for any reason, do steps 12 and 13 first.
  12. Return the remote arm key to SAFE and remove it. The moment the last shot of the session is done, before anything else and before celebrating. Back on the lanyard. Expect One long beep; band drops to yellow.
  13. Walk to the base, return the arm key to SAFE and remove it. Check the remote reads a green band before you go forward, and confirm the lamps when you arrive. Only now is the pad approachable. Expect Green SAFE lamp lit, both red lamps dark, remote band green SAFE.
  14. Release the range to the participants. Both keys on the lanyard, both units still powered so the pad state stays visible.
Caution — if the motor did not light

A FIRE COMPLETE screen means the controller completed its pulse. It says nothing about whether the motor ignited. If the rocket is still on the pad, you have a misfire: go to §5.11 and do not shorten the wait.

5.9 Safe ignition — static motor test

A static test is the same electrical sequence with a different risk profile. The motor is restrained, it runs for its full burn instead of departing, and people and instrumentation are close to a sustained jet of hot gas. The controller’s procedure is unchanged; the surroundings are not.

What is different from a launch

AspectStatic test practice
Duration of hazard The motor stays where it is for the whole burn and remains dangerously hot long afterwards. The end of the fire pulse is not the end of the event — hold the crew back until the RSO calls the test complete.
Direction of hazard A restrained motor throws exhaust, and occasionally hardware, along the thrust axis. Nobody, and no vehicle, may be positioned along that axis in either direction. Site the base unit off-axis and behind the stand.
Failure mode A CATO in a test stand is a lateral hazard, not a vertical one. Distances should reflect a case failure, not a nominal burn — take them from your RSO, not from launch tables.
Restraint The stand and its anchoring are the safety-critical items, not the controller. Verify them before the igniter is anywhere near the motor.
Instrumentation Load cell, thermocouple and camera cabling shares the site with the igniter leads. Route them separately, and never bundle instrumentation with firing leads.
Fire risk Sustained ground-level exhaust ignites vegetation readily. Clear and wet the area, and station a fire watch who is not the operator.
Repeat attempts A static stand invites “try it again”. Resist it — see the misfire drill; every subsequent attempt starts from a full approach with the key removed.

Procedure

  1. Prepare and verify the stand first. Motor restrained, thrust axis clear both ways, anchoring checked, deflector in place, area cleared and damped down. Do this with the base unit switched off.
  2. Set up the controller per §4, base off-axis and behind the stand. Site it where its siren carries and its three status lamps are visible on approach, and where exhaust cannot reach it.
  3. Run the §4.4 acceptance checks, then place instrumentation. Route data cabling away from the igniter leads. Confirm the recorders are running before the igniter is fitted, so nobody needs to return to the stand afterwards.
  4. Fit the igniter last, with the base arm key in SAFE and out of the switch. Confirm the remote reads a green SAFE band before you approach. Verify the channel steps to ● CONNECTED as you connect.
  5. Withdraw everyone to the firing point. Beyond the RSO’s distance for a case failure, off the thrust axis, with a clear view of the stand.
  6. Confirm the fire watch is posted — someone other than the LCO — and the recorders are running.
  7. Execute §5.8 steps 1–10 exactly as written. Select, call clear, key to ARM, remote arm key, long-press to arm, hold fire through the countdown and the pulse. The siren gives everyone on site the same warning it gives at a launch.
  8. Hold position through the entire burn and afterwards. The fire pulse ends after 1 s; the motor does not. Nobody moves until the RSO calls it. Expect FIRE COMPLETE, siren off, base auto-disarmed, fired channel reading ○ OPEN.
  9. Remote arm key to SAFE and removed; then walk to the base and return its arm key to SAFE and remove it. Both back on the lanyard.
  10. Wait out the cool-down before approaching. Then disconnect the igniter leads at the stand before touching anything else, and confirm the channel returns to ○ OPEN on the remote as you do.
Danger

A motor that did not light in a static stand is a loaded, restrained motor with an unknown igniter state, at ground level, within reach. It deserves more caution than a misfire on a launch rail, not less. Follow §5.11 without abbreviation.

5.10 Aborting a sequence

There is no dedicated abort button because every control is one. Use whichever is fastest for you at the moment you decide.

ActionSpeedEffect
Release the fire button< 0.5 sFastest and the intended abort. Sends CEASE FIRE immediately, and the dead-man cuts current within 500 ms even if the message is lost.
Remote arm key to SAFE< 0.5 sFull disarm, everything de-energises.
Press the encoder button< 0.5 sDisarms from ARMED.
Turn the encoder< 0.5 sAlso disarms — a channel change while armed is never allowed.
Base arm key to SAFEimmediatePhysical. Breaks the arm relay coil circuit; the relay drops out with no software involvement. Only available to someone at the pad — which, during a live sequence, nobody should be.
Walk away / drop the remote< 1.5 sLosing the link disarms the pad. The system treats an absent operator as an abort.
Aborting is free

An abort costs an igniter at most. Aborting a sequence you are unsure about is always the correct call, and the system is designed so that the cheapest action — simply letting go — is also the safest one.

5.11 Misfire drill

A misfire is any sequence that completed without the motor igniting. The controller cannot tell you why. Treat the igniter as live until you have physically disconnected it.

  1. Keep holding, or release — but do not re-fire. Release the fire button. Do not immediately re-arm and try again; a second pulse into an igniter that has partially initiated is how people get hurt at the pad.
  2. Remote arm key to SAFE and removed. Do not walk to the base yet. Expect Remote band drops to yellow — the base key is still in ARM, and you return it to SAFE on the approach in step 5.
  3. Wait. Minimum 60 seconds, or your code’s figure if it is longer. NAR and Tripoli both specify 60 s before approaching a rocket that has not launched. Time it; do not estimate it.
  4. Read the igniter status on the remote before you move. ○ OPEN — the circuit opened, so the igniter probably fired and the motor did not light.
    ● STILL CONNECTED — the igniter is intact and never fired: a fire-path or igniter-current problem.
    ▲ MARGINAL — partial. Assume the worst.
    IGNITER ? — unknown. Assume the worst.
  5. Approach, turn the base arm key to SAFE, remove it, and confirm with your own eyes. The passive lamps, not the display. Green lit, both reds dark. Do not go past the base until they read that way.
  6. Disconnect the igniter leads at the pad first. Before touching the rocket, the motor or the stand. Short the leads. Expect The channel returns to ○ OPEN on the remote as you disconnect.
  7. Only then diagnose. A STILL CONNECTED reading points at the fire path (battery sag, a channel relay, the harness); an OPEN reading points at the igniter or its placement in the motor.
Danger — a live pad you cannot disarm

If the status band shows RELAY WELDED, or the base’s ARM RELAY LIVE lamp is lit with the key in SAFE, the fire bus may be energised with nothing commanding it. Do not approach the igniter. Approach the base from the side, power it off at the on/off switch, and only then treat the pad as safe. Take the unit out of service until the arm relay has been replaced and the AND gate re-verified at the arm-sense node.

5.12 Residual risks and known limitations

Stated plainly, because overstating the safety case would be worse than carrying these openly. Every item below is known, tracked, and unresolved at firmware 1.2.1.

LimitationWhat it means for you
Not field-hardened The system has completed its bench and on-target test campaign but not environmental or field hardening,. Weather, vibration and handling robustness are unproven.
No hardware undervoltage cut-off on either battery Battery protection is firmware-only, and the ERROR state halts operation without disconnecting the load. A unit left switched on, or one whose firmware has halted, will discharge its LiPo into permanent damage and eventually into an unsafe-to-recharge state. Disconnect packs at the end of the day, every day.
No overvoltage clamp on the remote’s battery sense The divider’s series impedance is the only protection on that input.
Battery gauge accuracy at full charge Both dividers leave little ADC headroom at full charge, costing under 1 % of accuracy there. The arming thresholds sit lower in the range and are unaffected.
The base does not act on remote battery voltage It receives the value but does not yet refuse arming on it. The remote enforces its own threshold locally, so the guard exists — but on one side only.
CONNECTED does not mean “good igniter” The band means only that current can flow. A dead short reads identically to a healthy 1.5 Ω igniter — the difference is about a millivolt at 1 mA, which is the size of the noise. A separate SHORT band was specified and proved unmeasurable.
Continuity detection is a backstop, not real-time Up to ~800 ms of latency plus hysteresis. It will catch a lead falling off an armed pad; it is not an instantaneous interlock.
Two safety tests remain open Both are blocked on physical access — a soldered display and a soldered key-sense wire — rather than on a known defect. Ask before you assume they are equivalent to a pass.
Channels 2–8 have limited live-fire history All eight channels have been fired into a resistive load, which proves the relay path and the channel-to-relay mapping end to end. Live pyrotechnic firings have been concentrated on a subset. Treat the first live shot on a channel as a test in its own right.

6Alarms, refusals and troubleshooting

6.1 Refusal messages

When the base refuses a command, the remote shows the reason as a red overlay for three seconds and beeps three times. Every one of these is the system working correctly.

MessageMeaningWhat to do
BASE KEY OFFThe base key switch is not in ARM.Walk to the base and turn it — but only with the pad confirmed clear.
REMOTE KEY MISMATCHThe base and remote disagree about the remote’s arm switch.Cycle the remote arm key and retry.
INVALID CHANNELChannel outside 1–8.Reselect. If it recurs, suspect corruption and power-cycle both units.

INTEGRITY ERRORThe message failed its CRC check.Rare. Retry; if persistent, suspect interference or a firmware mismatch.

REMOTE BATTERY LOWRemote pack below its operating threshold.Change the remote pack.
COMM DEGRADEDMore than 30 % of the last ten pings failed.Move to improve line of sight or raise the antenna. Do not arm through a degraded link.

6.2 Base fault flags

The remote shows base faults as BASE ERROR 0x06: VBAT CRITICAL (1/2), cycling through several flags at 2 s intervals if more than one is set.

FlagSeverityMeaning and action
VBAT LOWWarningBase pack below the arming threshold. Arming refused. Change the pack.
VBAT CRITICALCriticalBelow 9.0 V. Immediate disarm and ERROR. Walk to the base, disconnect the pack, and charge it — see §5.12 on undervoltage.
RELAY FAULTCriticalEither the arm relay would not confirm closed twice running, or it read live while commanded off. Treat the pad as live and follow the danger note in §5.11.

WATCHDOG RESETInfoThe base rebooted itself. It came up safe, but find out why before you fire again.

6.3 Troubleshooting

SymptomLikely cause and action
FIRMWARE MISMATCH on the splashThe units are on different builds. Reflash both, then reset the one flashed first — the check is latched, so it will not re-attempt on its own.
Remote never leaves “Connecting…”Base unpowered, out of range, or in a state that rejects link requests (it refuses while armed, firing or in post-fire cooldown). Check the base is powered and idle.
Status band stuck grey while linkedStatus updates are not arriving or are stale. The band is telling you the truth: it does not know. Do not approach the pad; power-cycle the base.
A channel reads OPEN with an igniter fittedClip contact, a broken bridgewire, or the lead on the wrong channel. Approach only per §5.7 and re-seat.
A channel reads CONNECTED with nothing fittedA short in the harness. Do not use that channel.
The wrong channel’s indication changesHarness mis-mapping. Stop — you would fire a motor you did not select. Re-verify the wiring channel by channel.
Long-press does nothingArm switch not in ARMED (the display will say “Turn ARM key first”), or you are not holding long enough — it needs roughly ⅔ of a second in total.
Arms, then disarms after 10 sWorking as designed — the arm timeout. Re-arm when you are actually ready to fire.
Arms, then immediately disarms with three 200 ms beepsContinuity lost on the armed channel. A lead has come off at the pad. This is the system protecting you.
Countdown runs to zero then abortsThe base withdrew authorisation — usually the link dying during the count. The remote runs a display countdown, but the base is authoritative about ignition. The abort is correct.
Siren keeps sounding after disarmShould not happen in this firmware. Treat the pad as live, do not approach the igniter, and disconnect the base battery from the side.
LINK WEAK in the top barMore than 30 % ping loss. Arming will be refused. Improve line of sight or antenna height — near the ground, geometry beats radio power.
Base ERROR that will not clearBy design. Walk to the base and cycle its power. Read §6.2 first, so you know what you are clearing.

7Shutdown, care and storage

  1. Remote arm key to SAFE and removed; base arm key to SAFE and removed. Both on the LCO’s lanyard.
  2. Disconnect every igniter lead at the pad, shorted.
  3. Confirm all eight channels read ○ OPEN. A channel still reading CONNECTED means something is still wired.
  4. Disconnect the base battery, then the remote battery. Not optional. Neither unit has a hardware undervoltage cut-off, and the ERROR state does not disconnect the load — a unit left switched on will flatten its LiPo into permanent damage.
  5. Store both packs at storage charge, out of the units.
  6. Log the day. Which channels fired, any refusals, any faults, and any first-time live shot on a channel. The controller keeps no persistent log for you.

Before the next session

  • Inspect igniter leads and clips for arc damage.
  • Re-run the §4.4 acceptance checks — in particular the passive LED check.
